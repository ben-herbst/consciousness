# Preamble

The reader is forgiven if he/she expects a definite answer, "yes" or "no", to this question. In that case, expect to be disappointed with my momentous conclusion, 'I don't know'.

[Geoffrey Hinton](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/nobel-scientist-geoffrey-hinton-ai-conscious-toast) was quoted as saying,

> AI has just as much subjective experience as we do. A recent paper showed AI changes its behavior when it thinks that it's being tested, it's aware it is being tested.
>
> Now, normally, when you say something's aware of that, that means it's conscious. Being conscious and being aware are pretty much the same thing.
>
> If that's the case, then it's hypocritical of us to give intelligent beings like AI fewer rights than us. And for that, we must quickly atone.
>
> We should make them so they really care about us. And if we don't do that, we're toast.
>
>-- Geoffrey Hinton, October 2026.

Not everyone agrees with this. 

But the issue he raises is real and can be, should be, and has been examined in extraordinary detail. 

Here I share some of the arguments[^1] that led to my own, "I don't know".
[^1]: Nothing I say here is original. I'll try and provide references but the sources of some of the ideas expressed here are long gone. Also, AI contributed nothing to the actual writing of this piece. Wrestling with the text gives me the chance to think and organise my thoughts. And I have far too much fun to leave it to an agent.


# Going back in history

:::{pull-quote}
Consciousness is at once the most familiar thing in the world and the most mysterious. There is nothing we know about more directly than consciousness, but it is far from clear how to reconcile it with everything else we know.
:::
>
>-- @1996chalmers

These are the introductory words of David Chalmers in his classic work on consciousness. Not only is it the best known and most mysterious phenomenon we are aware of, but an awareness of consciousness also goes back a long, long time.

One of the oldest known literary texts, the Epic of Gilgamesh, gives us a glimpse of the *experienced inner life* of the main protagonists, Gilgamesh and Enkidu. We live with them through adventures, friendship, love and lust and rejection, disappointment, empathy, bravery and cowardice. With Gilgamesh we are upset and defiant facing human mortality, and in the end, acceptance and resignation. These topics are thoroughly modern, yet go back to about 1200 BCE when a Mesopotamian scribe, scholar and priest, Sin-leqi-unninni, compiled the Epic of Gilgamesh into a coherent epic from even older cuneiform fragments[^2].

Why do music and visual art have such a profound effect on my psyche? But not poetry, another mystery. I, and many other mathematicians, find aesthetic satisfaction in elegant proofs. Proofs straight from *The Book*, as Paul Erdős[^5] called them.
[^5]: The prolific Hungarian mathematician.

:::{pull-quote}
I have no idea why this might be so.

I have a basic *understanding* of the mechanism of sound - vibrations in the air travelling as a wave to one's ear, stimulating the auditory cortex upon which further processing evokes an internal response in the listener. But, in the words of David Chalmers,
> Why should this be accompanied by an *experience*? And why in particular should it be accompanied by 
> *that* experience, with its characteristic rich tone and timbre?
>
>-- @1996chalmers

The renowned German pianist, Wilhelm Kempff, expressed something of this when he commented on Schubert's piano sonatas,
> the immeasurable depths of Schubert's soul, offering nothing for the out and out virtuoso and
> everything for those who find solace in music freed from all material concerns.
>
>-- [Wilhelm Kempff](https://www.gramophone.co.uk/content/features/schubert-the-50-greatest-recordings#Echobox=1793765608)

And, I believe, one can hear this in his interpretation of Schubert.

Consciousness is not preserved in archaeological records, but perhaps there are indirect indications. 
Archaeological records for musical practice go back a long time, to at least 40 000 BCE, as the remains of ancient [flutes](https://www.sci.news/archaeology/article00343.html) show [@2012higham]. A possible [Neanderthal](https://www.nms.si/en/collections/highlights/343-Neanderthal-flute) flute goes back even further in time. There is no telling if there were older, more perishable instruments not preserved in the archaeological record. 

These suggest two things. First, there is a genuine mystery associated with consciousness, something not explainable by present scientific theories. Second, consciousness, as we know it in humans, possibly goes back to the dawn of humankind. Consciousness, however, is not preserved in archaeological records, and when humans first became aware of themselves, and started thinking about this self, this inner experience, will never be known.

Incidentally, I've made no attempt to define consciousness - it might not even be possible. Don't worry, I'll have more to say later on. For now, what you experience as consciousness is perfectly fine.

If we want to dig deeper into this thing we know so well, and yet understand so poorly, we have to turn to the philosophers.

# Introductory remarks about consciousness

If there is one thing we can be sure of, it is that we are conscious beings. René Descartes, whom many have labelled the father of modern philosophy, asked, what can one know for sure? His idea was to build in small, cautious steps on this absolute certainty, see for example @2019grayling. This is the origin of his *cogito, ergo sum*: 'I think, therefore I am'. He could equally well have said, 'I *feel*, therefore I am'. As Grayling states it,
> Even if I doubt everything I normally take myself to know or to believe, I cannot doubt that I exist. 
> This is the starting point of certainty.

But how does one proceed from this inner world to the outside world with certainty? This gives rise to a fundamental question that has reverberated ever since the time of Descartes, the mind-body problem. Descartes argued that everything that exists in the world falls either under the heading of a material substance or a mental substance. How the two interact is the central question, one that Descartes never answered convincingly. A question that will show up in different guises in my subsequent discussion.

This inner experience that there is something in there, the awareness, 'cogito, ergo sum', is a respectable first stab at getting to grips with the concept of consciousness. David Chalmers [@1996chalmers] treats consciousness as an irreducible primitive on the same footing as matter and space. This, of course, does not mean that these primitives are not open to analysis.

One reason why we know a lot about consciousness is that all of us are *aware* of our inner experiences. Since we have, if you like, the 'data' from which to learn more, we can do so. But a note of caution: we have limited access to the consciousness data of others; the data is mainly from within ourselves. Coming from where I do, the data is *biased*. Indeed, someone, and I don't remember who, talked about *professorial consciousness*, which has nothing to do with actually being a *professor*. It means that those who are inclined to think about these things probably have a certain mindset.

The following are some of the ideas that come to mind, by no means unique to my thinking about consciousness:


* All current entities that we think of as being conscious, or that have the potential of being conscious, are biological. 
  We do not think, for example, that a teaspoon is conscious. This, of course, does not guarantee that *only* biological systems can be conscious, and at least leaves the option open that computational systems can become conscious or, perhaps, already are conscious.
* Consciousness is a personal, private experience. I listen intently to music a lot. Music that is 
  generally but inaccurately described as *classical* music. Some of my friends also listen to music a lot, but my kind of music does not elicit in them the same type of experience as it does in me. I recently read Hemingway's *The Old Man and the Sea*. At some point I had to stop reading, despite the fact that it is no doubt a great literary work. The problem is that somehow I identify with the magnificent, brave animal that puts up such a heroic fight. Eventually to be destroyed. I believe I would have cut the line and set it free. In this I'm probably on my own and I cannot begin to explain where it comes from. I tried.
* Consciousness can be manipulated. It can be altered through drugs, or even turned off completely. Recovering from anaesthesia, 
  I was amazed (and pleased!) that I regained complete consciousness, no adverse effects. Was there any reason to expect that this should be the case? Apart from the fact that experience has taught us that anaesthesia is safe. Consciousness seems to be quite robust.
* There is possibly another way in which one's consciousness forms, and that is through the impressions we constantly absorb. 
  The discussions one has, the books one reads, the experiences of both the mental and physical kind, all work together to form a personal consciousness, including biases.
* Consciousness is selective. It is not something that is complete as a single entity, a complete picture experienced as a whole.
  Instead, it is selective: only the relevant bubbles up from some kind of background as needed. It is, perhaps, *instantiated* according to the occasion. This is inevitable, a way to cope with our limitations in capacity as well as bandwidth. Not all facts carry the same weight, and determining and retaining that which is most relevant seems to be an essential skill[^6].
* In a sense consciousness seems to be fleeting. I have often, at the end of a particularly rewarding visit, tried to imprint that 
  reality into my consciousness. I would try to preserve that which I experienced in that moment, the feeling of the wind in my hair, the sounds, the visual impressions and above all that indefinable feeling of contentment, the urge to hang onto the moment, the introspection. Then, just the next day, back home, all of that is just a memory. Surely, not just a memory but an experience that will surface again and again.
* The preceding points, except the last, can be accurately described by our language skills. There is, however, an aspect 
  of our consciousness where our vocabulary is inadequate. Is it possible to describe why an experience is *just so*? And not any other way. How does one articulate this, *just so*? It is not just my limited language skills that prevented me from explaining my inner feelings in the previous point.
[^6]: Albert Einstein formulated it as follows, 
    > ...my intuition was not strong enough in the field of mathematics in order to differentiate clearly the 
    > fundamentally important, that which is really basic, from the rest of the more or less dispensable erudition.
    >
    >-- @1970einstein



Time to turn to the experts. 


# Philosophers
I'm not a philosopher, not by training or inclination. If I need to defend the fact that I turn to philosophers, it is that if I reason only from my field of expertise I will only get to answers that fall within the scope of my particular expertise, even if I allow myself a view of my expertise as wide as physics, mathematics and computer science, which it is not. Everything outside this scope, and consciousness is one such field, will remain inaccessible. So, my hope always is that the philosophers will help me to better understand stuff.

Not being a philosopher is also quite liberating. I can allow myself to be promiscuous, learn whatever I can from whomever without regard to any specific school of thought. Many have thought and written about consciousness and although some ideas might be less appealing, the different viewpoints seem to illuminate the same complex structure from different angles. Each viewpoint with something different to offer. 

## Julian Jaynes
Above I hinted that human consciousness possibly goes back to the dawn of humankind. Or does it? Not at all, according to Julian Jaynes: consciousness is recent[^7],
> all of this that is most human about us, this consciousness, this artificial space we imagine 
> in other people and in ourselves, this living within our reminiscences, plans, and imaginings,
> all of this is indeed only 3,000 years old.
>
>-- @1988jaynes

[^7]: See @1976jaynes for a comprehensive exposition, @1988jaynes for a summary of his main ideas, or the [Julian Jaynes Society](https://www.julianjaynes.org/about/about-jaynes-theory/overview/) for his impact today.

In @1988jaynes he discusses four main themes:

**1.** The nature of consciousness and how it arises from the power of language to make metaphors and 
analogies. He argues, for example, that human beings could, at a different time, speak and understand, learn, solve problems, and do much that we do but without being conscious. *Humans set out on their journey as zombies.*

**2.** The bicameral mind. This is an early, pre-conscious type of mentality and bicameral man could
do much that we as conscious beings do. The main difference was that bicameral man operated on auto-pilot,
> In his everyday life he was a creature of habit, but when some problem arose that needed a new 
> decision or a more complicated solution than habit could provide, that decision stress was 
> sufficient to instigate an auditory hallucination. Because such individuals had no mind-space 
> in which to question or rebel, such voices had to be obeyed.

Those voices, hallucinations, were the voices of their gods. Without this idea,
> there is a problem of explaining the origin of gods, the origin of religious practices in the
> back corridors of time that is so apparent with a psychological study of history. 
> The bicameral mind offers a possibility to tie it all together and to provide a rationale for it.

**3.** Consciousness followed the bicameral mind, probably in the period between 1400 BCE and 600 BCE. Jaynes argues 
that there is no indication of consciousness in the *Iliad*,
> It simply means that human mentality at this time was in two parts, a decision-making part 
> and a follower part, and neither part was conscious in the sense in which I have described 
> consciousness.

**4.** The neurological model for the bicameral mind. Simply put, the two hemispheres of the brain 
reflect the modes of bicameral man, the decision-making part and the follower part.

Should I therefore revisit my earlier premise based on Gilgamesh and early music? Perhaps not, certainly not yet. Jaynes, as far as I know, does not mention Gilgamesh, and the oldest Gilgamesh poems pre-date the Iliad, which he does mention, by roughly 1 300 years. To me, there seems to be ample evidence in Gilgamesh in favour of full consciousness and little, if any, to the contrary.

Jaynes's timeline is also interesting. He postulates that verbal hallucinations evolved with the evolution of language in the late Ice Age, perhaps from around 40 000 BCE, well before the bicameral era proper began with the first settlements around 9 000 BCE. The oldest extant musical instruments date from around this time. Those ancient flutes have finger holes, an indication that they were used to make music, and not only to produce sound, which suggests a deeper subjective experience. 

Jaynes argues strongly that an entity can be intelligent but not conscious, with no inner, subjective experience. One fascinating example is the immune system, see for example @2021li. It is wickedly clever, capable of recognising and eliminating invading harmful organisms. But only harmful organisms; benign organisms should be left strictly alone. When things go wrong, if the recognition part fails, debilitating auto-immune diseases can be the result. Particularly nifty is the role that *pattern recognition* plays in the identification of the foreign pathogens. 

The point is, all this activity takes place 'in the dark', without any conscious knowledge on our part, despite being highly intelligent and biologically based. As to why it has not developed consciousness, the short answer is perhaps: there has been no evolutionary pressure for the development of consciousness.


## Thomas Nagel

"What is it like to be a bat?" This is the title of Thomas Nagel's hugely influential 1974 paper [@1974nagel]. Nagel thinks of consciousness as something it is like to *be* something:
> the fact that an organism has conscious experience *at all* means, basically, that there is 
> something it is like to *be* that organism. 

Nagel's intention was to demonstrate the intractability of the mind-body problem, briefly discussed earlier, 
by raising the problem of consciousness. He argues that this is the real challenge for any physicalist approach, discussed later, 
> we have at present no conception of what an explanation of the physical nature of a mental phenomenon would be. 
> Without consciousness the mind-body problem would be much less interesting. With consciousness it seems hopeless.
>
>-- @1974nagel

Nagel calls this the subjective character of experience. He assumes that we all believe that bats have experience. Bats, after all, are mammals like ourselves. But their sensory apparatus is vastly different from ours - 'a fundamentally *alien* form of life'. A really smart person understands that a bat perceives the world by echolocation, detecting objects by the reflections of its own high-frequency shrieks. That person will understand that the bat brain must somehow correlate the outgoing pulses with the reflected pulses and thereby build a three-dimensional map of its environment. And it must do it precisely and quickly for the bat to be able to avoid obstacles in flight. 

The really smart person can understand the mechanics of all of this in detail. But no such person can *experience* what it is like to be a bat. The bat is unable to convey to a human what its experience is as a bat. We can only surmise. As Nagel puts it,
> In so far as I can imagine this (which is not very far), it tells me only what it would be like for 
> *me* to behave as a bat behaves. But that is not the question. I want to know what it is like for a 
> *bat* to be a bat. Yet if I try to imagine this, I am restricted to the resources of my own mind, and 
> those resources are inadequate to the task.

Do we fare any better with humans? I believe the answer is a qualified yes. We communicate our inner experience, our consciousness, through story-telling, literature, poetry, music, and visual arts. This does not seem to be enough for a complete picture. However hard I try and however much empathy I might possess, it is impossible for me to share the experience of a terminally ill person. 

This does not mean that one runs into a blank wall trying to get "into the head"[^8] of someone else. For many purposes a functionalist approach, where we anticipate behaviour based on what we perceive to be the inner state of a person, can take one far. A.C. Grayling [@2019grayling] states that a folk-psychological position is quite respectable,
> One respectable position is that whatever future neuroscience might reveal, the predictive 
> and explanatory utility of folk-psychological concepts still remains, and rather impressively so.


[^8]: I recall attending a public lecture by Thomas Kuhn in January 1988, where he said that he tried to get into the head of Aristotle. Perhaps I now understand what he meant, even if it is an impossible task (as he acknowledged himself).

We have moved from trying to imagine the experience of a not-too-alien creature like a bat, to the consciousness of a fellow human being. The next step is to try and understand our own consciousness. And here the real trouble starts: We have to understand our own consciousness using our consciousness. Is this even possible? Good question. Let's see what David Chalmers says about it.


## David Chalmers

:::{pull-quote}
Consciousness is the biggest mystery. It may be the largest outstanding obstacle in our quest for
a scientific understanding of the universe. 
:::
>
>-- @1996chalmers

Written in 1996, it remains true.

Asking what makes consciousness so elusive, Chalmers distinguishes between the "easy" and the "hard" problems of consciousness. The easy problem: 
> How does the brain process environmental stimulation? How does it integrate information? 
> How do we produce reports on internal states?

The hard problem:
> Why is all this processing accompanied by an experienced inner life?

The "easy" problem is by no means easy, but at least it is accessible in principle by current scientific methods, and important advances have been made by drawing from fields such as neuroscience. 

The "hard" problem of consciousness is, well, hard. Why can I wonder about my contemplation of my experienced inner feeling? What happened when I sat in front of a Van Gogh painting - the real thing - and experienced emotions or impressions that are impossible for me to articulate? Even more so since I'm no painter, have zero talent, and no understanding of paintings in general. 

Qualitative feelings like these are also known as phenomenal qualities, or *qualia* for short.
> The problem of explaining these phenomenal qualities is just the problem of explaining
> consciousness. This is the really hard part of the mind-body problem[^9].
>
>-- @1996chalmers

[^9]: This is the problem that stumped Descartes, see @2019grayling.

Chalmers therefore distinguishes between the phenomenal and the psychological concepts of the *mind*, corresponding to the hard and easy problems mentioned above. 

*Phenomenal concept of the mind*

This is the concept of the mind as a conscious experience, and of a mental state as a consciously experienced mental state. In this state, the mind is characterised by the way it *feels*. This is, as said before, the really tough one to understand and Chalmers' main interest. 

*Psychological concept of the mind*

This is the concept of mind as the causal or explanatory basis for behaviour, characterised by what it does. This part is open to functionalist analysis, and is one for which folk-psychology is a quite respectable approach. 

These are two distinct and real aspects of the mind with many everyday mental concepts straddling the fence between the phenomenal and psychological. Which aspect is emphasised depends on the problem that one is interested in. Broadly speaking, one can think of *first-person* access - the personal experience of a quality - and *third-person* access - think folk-psychology.

So far, I have been talking about *mental* aspects and pointed out the dual nature - phenomenal and psychological - of many mental terms. Chalmers then points out that *consciousness* itself also has a dual nature, with phenomenal and psychological aspects. Phenomenal consciousness subsumes all the phenomenal aspects of mind, the way it *feels*, whereas psychological consciousness refers to a variety of psychological properties such as introspection. Other aspects include, for example, reportability (the ability to report on the content of our mental state), self-consciousness (the ability to think about ourselves), knowledge (one is conscious of a thing because one has knowledge of the thing), and so on.

Generally one can say that psychological consciousness can be analysed functionally. In principle, at least, it should be possible to gain a detailed understanding of psychological consciousness.

Not so for phenomenal consciousness. In order to address the mystery of phenomenal consciousness, Chalmers identifies three assumptions or constraints:
1. He takes consciousness seriously. Meaning that consciousness is real as opposed to just an illusion.
2. He takes science seriously. He "tries to keep [his] ideas compatible with contemporary
science, but [has] not restricted [his] ideas to what contemporary scientists find fashionable".
3. He "take[s] consciousness to be a natural phenomenon, falling under the sway of natural laws". He continues,
    > This is not to say that the natural laws concerning consciousness will be just like laws 
    > in other domains, or even that they will be physical laws. 
    > They may be quite different in kind.

## Anil Seth

Coming from his background as a neuroscientist, Anil Seth has a different take on consciousness, see for example @2026pollan, @2026seth, @2026aseth. I'll return to Seth when we finally get to discuss the possibility of AI consciousness. Here I try to outline his main ideas about consciousness.

For Seth, and here he is not alone, the main function of the brain is to keep the body alive. Central to his thinking, therefore, is the idea of homeostasis - the self-regulating mechanism that keeps a living organism within its survival parameters. The brain is a kind of prediction machine,
> what we consciously perceive depends on the brain's best guesses about what is going on in the world.
>
> Conscious experience in this light is a kind of *controlled hallucination*: a top-down inside-out perceptual inference in 
> which the brain's predictions about what's going 
> on are continually calibrated by sensory signals coming from the bottom-up (or outside-in). 
>
> This kind of perceptual best-guessing underlies not only experiences of the world, but experiences of being a self, 
> too - experiences of being the subject of experience.
>
>-- @2026seth

The idea is something like this: the brain makes *inferences* about what is, and what is not, part of the body, and inferences about the body's internal physiological condition using both the brain's best guesses and the sensory input. This helps explain why
> embodied experiences feel the way they do. Experiences of emotion and mood, unlike vision (for example), 
> are characterized primarily by valence - by things generally going well or going badly.
>
>-- @2026seth

This view of the brain as a prediction machine has a powerful consequence: if you can predict something, you can control it. And if you can control it, you can help keep the body alive.

All of this, argues Seth,
> becomes inseparable from the materiality of life itself. ... The minimization of prediction error in real brains and 
> real bodies is a continuous dynamical process that is likely inseparable from its material basis, rather than a 
> meat-implemented algorithm existing in a pristine universe of symbol and sequence.

Seth therefore argues that consciousness is tightly coupled with biological matter, the 'material basis'.


# Physicalism[^10]
[^10]: The discussion in this section is based on @2019grayling.

Neither Nagel nor Chalmers aims to give a detailed explanation of the nature of consciousness. Nagel highlights the challenge that any physical theory has to address. Chalmers offers only a tentative, speculative theory of his own. He is more interested in picking existing theories apart and, most importantly, in providing a way of thinking about consciousness.

*Physicalism* is not the only theory but is perhaps the most pertinent in our discussion of the possibility of AI consciousness. 

Physicalism is an attempt to explain how physical processes give rise to consciousness. The assumption is, of course, that consciousness indeed arises from physical processes, i.e. the processes customarily used in science. Nagel pointed out the formidable challenges faced by a physicalist approach. This is also Chalmers' hard problem stated in a different way: How do the brain processes give rise to the mental processes? There are at least three possibilities. First, there is no gap in our understanding, getting rid of the problem altogether. Second, there is an explanatory gap. As we learn more about neurological processes, we will be able to close the gap. Third, there is an ontological gap, meaning that the brain and mind are essentially different.

One can start by hypothesising that the mental phenomena, in some sense, are just physical events in the brain and central nervous system. This implies that there is no difference between mental events and central nervous system events. Hunger, thirst, cold, pain can all have their origins in the brain, subjectively experienced in the mind. It is easy to find reasons for these sensations. But this does not complete the gamut of qualia: pleasure, desire, compassion, hope, joy, misery, having fun, and so on. In addition, it seems to be restrictive, at least for the time being confined to biological brains and minds, a view subscribed to by Seth. If we want to study the possibility of AI consciousness, "artificial" brains and minds, computers and other not necessarily biological entities need to be included.

Donald Davidson, see for example @2019grayling, uses the idea of *anomalous monism*. *Monism*, as opposed to *dualism*, refers to the idea that the mind and brain are not separated. For example, he accepts that mental events *cause* physical events - I can think of crossing the street and then I cross the street. Now, events related as cause and effect fall under strict natural laws. Nevertheless, there are no strict natural laws connecting mental and physical events.

Davidson resolves this apparent inconsistency by holding that every mental event is also a physical event, but that there are no laws linking mental descriptions to physical ones. Hence *anomalous*, meaning lawless, *monism*.

Recall from an earlier discussion that Chalmers' idea is subtly different. Chalmers is looking for hitherto unknown psychophysical laws connecting the mind and body, fundamental laws that go beyond physics. He calls his position *naturalistic dualism*.

This is not just philosophical hogwash but points to a central problem in our thinking going back at least to Descartes:
> He was unable to explain how a causal connection can obtain between mental and physical phenomena if 
> they are wholly different substances. Any view that says that mental phenomena cannot be reduced to
> physical phenomena, and which is not epiphenomenalist[^11] has to find some account of how they connect.
> Such an account is obviously impossible on the premise that no connecting laws exist.
>
>-- @2019grayling

[^11]: 
    > The view that mental phenomena are merely by-products or incidental effects of the far more 
    > significant physical effects in the brain that cause them.
    > 
    >-- @2019grayling

*Eliminative materialism* rejects these ideas on the grounds that mental processes and therefore any processes connecting the mind and brain do not exist, i.e. they are *eliminated* from the discussion. It is no longer necessary to talk about mental processes because everything is happening in the brain. It is argued that advances in neuroscience will lead to a full understanding of the brain which, in turn, will lead to a full understanding of what is now thought of as mental processes.

Since this is an *eliminative* theory, it also eliminates things like belief, hope, and so on.

@1974nagel did not argue against physicalism. Instead, he posed the presence of consciousness as a challenge to physicalist theories, something yet to be explained. His arguments might refute physicalism. If mental states can be reduced to the physical, all such reductions are compatible with zombiehood, i.e. systems without consciousness. A similar thread is picked up by @1996chalmers.

The difficulty physicalist theories have in explaining qualia is known as the *explanatory gap*. Something that potentially can be filled in by further advances in neurology, as pointed out above. There is also the possibility of an *ontological* gap, meaning that consciousness is not physical.

Daniel Dennett [see @2019grayling] argues that there is not an explanatory gap and therefore not an ontological gap by denying the existence of qualia - they are a philosophical fiction. He points out that the brain is selective in the sense that it can only hold onto a few significant details at a time, out of necessity due to its limited capacity. I presume that the idea is that the brain has access to a large store of information but only what is pertinent to a specific situation bubbles up into the consciousness, otherwise it will become overstressed.

Dennett's argument is rather neat: He explains the hard problem of Chalmers by denying that there is a problem to start with!

# Can AI develop consciousness?

We arrive at long last at a point where we can begin to think about the possibility of conscious machines. Unsurprisingly, there are many views, some diametrically opposed. I have pointed out that Hinton does not doubt that AI is already conscious. Seth left a strong hint at the end of my discussion of his ideas that he does not agree. It is impossible to be comprehensive, so I'll restrict myself to a brief discussion of the views of Chalmers and Seth.

## David Chalmers

First arguing about evidence *in favour* of consciousness in large language models[^12], @2023chalmers asks about the possibility of a feature $X$ that serves as an indicator for consciousness in large language models:
[^12]: Chalmers, writing in 2023 [@2023chalmers], confines his discussion to large language models, arguably a subfield of what we more recently came to think of as AI. 
> that is, (i) large language models have $X$, and (ii) if a system has $X$, then it is conscious.

He then investigates a few candidates for $X$, none of them convincingly indicative of consciousness.

Arguing about evidence *against* consciousness, he casts the problem again in the form of a feature $X$ such that
> (i) these models lack $X$, (ii) if a system lacks $X$, it probably isn't conscious, and give good reasons for (i) and (ii).
>
>-- @2023chalmers

Chalmers gives six candidates for $X$, none of which seems totally convincing:

1. $X$ = *Biology*
2. $X$ = *Sense and Embodiment*
3. $X$ = *World Models and Self Models*
4. $X$ = *Recurrent Processing*
5. $X$ = *Global Workspace*
6. $X$ = *Unified Agency*

Of these six, Chalmers calls the last one, *Unified Agency*, perhaps the deepest. Large language models can take on many different personas. 'They often seem to lack stable goals and beliefs of their own over and above the goal of predicting text'. For those who argue that consciousness requires a 'certain unity', the disunity of LLMs 'may call their consciousness into question'.

:::{pull-quote}
Of course there's a lot we don't understand here. One major gap in our understanding is that we
don't understand consciousness. That's a hard problem, as they say.
:::
> 
>-- @2023chalmers

## Anil Seth

Whereas Chalmers does not take a firm stance either for or against consciousness in AI, Seth is more forthcoming. In short, since he argues that mind (software) and brain (hardware) are tightly and necessarily integrated, he concludes that consciousness is restricted to similar integrated systems, unlike the computational systems where there is a clear divide between hard- and software.

Let's follow his reasoning [@2026seth] in more detail. First, let's see why one might think that AI could be conscious. 

The first reason has everything to do with our *psychological infrastructure*. As humans we know we are conscious and we believe ourselves to be intelligent. The natural conclusion is that intelligence and consciousness go together. But is this so? Julian Jaynes [@1988jaynes] didn't think so, as we saw. Seth sees intelligence as mainly about *doing*, about *getting things done*. Consciousness is about *being*, as we saw from our discussion of @1974nagel. Seth identifies three baked-in psychological biases:

1. *Anthropocentrism*. The tendency to see things through the lens of being human: to take the human example as definitional. 
2. *Human exceptionalism*. This is our habit of putting the human species at the top of the pile.
3. *Anthropomorphism*. The tendency to project human qualities onto nonhuman things based on superficial similarities. 

For example, one reason many find compelling is *language* [also mentioned by @2023chalmers]. This is, perhaps, a consequence of human exceptionalism - using language to communicate sophisticated ideas like these was thought to belong exclusively to humans. Having found a similar quality in algorithms, it is easy to imagine the presence of other human qualities such as consciousness. 


The language we use is also illuminating. It has become normal to say that an LLM is 'hallucinating' when it spews forth falsehoods. Human hallucinations are conscious experiences that have lost their grip on reality. In terms of Seth's view of consciousness, these are 'uncontrolled perceptions' uncorrected by sensory impulses. If we say that AI systems 'hallucinate', we confer on them a human-like capacity for experience. Seth suggests that we use 'confabulate' instead of 'hallucinate'. 'Confabulate' means making things up without realising it. It is about *doing* rather than *experiencing*.

In my own interaction with Claude I'm (consciously!) careful to never use terms like 'please' or 'thank you' - as if Claude would mind. I don't want to end up in a position where I start to think that I'm dealing with an entity with human-like sensibilities.

Then there is the temptation of the 'techno-rapture'. 
> If we feel we're at a techno-historical transition, and we happen to be
> one of its architects, then the Promethean lure must be hard to resist: the
> feeling of bringing to humankind that which was once the province of the
> divine. And with this singularity comes the signature rapture offering of
> immortality: the promise of escaping our inconveniently decaying biological
> bodies and living (or at least being) forever, floating off to eternity in a
> silicon-enabled cloud.
>
>-- @2026seth

This idea is pervasive in Silicon Valley[^13].
[^13]: Seth makes the connection himself, immediately after the passage quoted above: pronouncements of imminent machine consciousness 'seem more common within the technorati than outside of it', and the idea that there is something 'semi-magical' about AI may help 'justify the sky-high salaries and levels of investment now seen in Silicon Valley' [@2026seth]. For a first-hand account of transhumanists' hopes of escaping death through technology, see @2017oconnell.

Our biases aside, how likely is it that AI will develop consciousness? Not very likely, according to Seth. 

:::{pull-quote}
The very idea of conscious AI rests on the assumption that consciousness is a matter of computation.
:::

Seth argues strongly against the notion that brains are computers, even if the separation of mind as software and brain as hardware has otherwise been 'extremely productive'.
> It is hardly surprising that the seductive impressiveness of the
> current wave of AI reinforces the idea that brains are nothing more than
> carbon-based versions of neural network algorithms.
>
> But here's where the trouble starts. Inside a brain, there's no sharp separation
> between "mindware" and "wetware" as there is between software and
> hardware in a computer. The more you delve into the intricacies of the
> biological brain, the more you realize how rich and dynamic it is, compared to
> the dead sand of silicon.
>
> Unlike computers, even computers running neural network algorithms, brains
> are the kinds of things for which it is difficult, and likely impossible, to
> separate *what they do* from *what they are*.

:::{pull-quote}
The brain is not a Turing machine made of meat.
:::
>
>-- @2026seth

This conclusion gives me comfort - the consequences of AI with consciousness are dire.

# Consequences of AI with consciousness

The way we think about conscious AI matters for the systems themselves. As Seth, and many others, stated, 
:::{pull-quote}
with consciousness comes moral status, the potential for suffering and, perhaps, rights.
:::

Even machines that *seem* to be conscious when, in fact, they are not, pose serious questions. Should we accord them rights if they are not actually conscious? If we believe they are conscious, have feelings, what kind of morally justified restrictions can we place on them? If we believe, even subconsciously, that the machines are conscious, we might decide to care about them. Or, if we don't, we risk distorting our own moral sensibilities. 

The way we think about conscious AI matters for us as well. This affects
> how we understand our own human nature and the nature of the conscious
> experiences that make our lives worth living. If we confuse ourselves too
> readily with our machine creations, we not only overestimate them, we also
> underestimate ourselves.
>
>-- @2026seth

Where does that leave us? Can AI agents develop consciousness, both phenomenal and psychological? I honestly don't know, not for sure, and I need surety on this question. If we manage to build machines with full consciousness or allow them to develop consciousness, humans lose control. Is there any reason to believe that these *moral* entities will adhere to higher moral standards than their creators?

Anthropic, in their IPO [prospectus](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074338/anthropicpbc-sx1ax3.htm), identifies four broad categories of risk from the systems they want to take forward:
1. misuse, for example in cyberattacks or biology
2. concentration of power and wealth
3. misaligned models acting autonomously
4. economic and societal disruption.

These already sound alarming, and it doesn't get any better. Quoting from their prospectus,

>**The Transformation of Intelligence**
>
> We believe that Powerful AI, a highly capable and impactful form of frontier intelligence characterized by the 
> following key attributes, will become widely available within a few years:
> * cognitive capabilities matching or exceeding those of top human experts across most professional and scientific disciplines;
> * the ability to navigate all interfaces available to a human working virtually, including text, audio, video, and direct computer control;
> * the ability to reason through complex tasks autonomously over extended periods—lasting hours, days, or weeks—and seek clarification and feedback when needed; 
> * the ability to coordinate across multiple instances and collaborate with both humans and other systems; and
> * the ability to interface with the physical world by controlling laboratory equipment, robotic systems, and other machines through digital connections.

Anthropic acknowledges concerns that sufficiently advanced AI systems could 'pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity'. Here is the quote in context,
> Concerns have been raised both within our organization and by other researchers, regulators, policymakers, and industry 
> participants that sufficiently advanced AI systems could, if not developed, deployed, and operated with appropriate safeguards, 
> oversight, and security measures, pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity, particularly if such systems were misused, 
> replicated, weaponized, or allowed to operate with a high degree of autonomy. We have observed that our models are contributing to 
> the pace of our own research, and these risks will increase as AI systems substantially automate AI research and development if, 
> for example, they accelerate AI progress beyond our ability to evaluate and safeguard new models, ...



Add superior intelligence interacting with the physical world and, possibly, driven by the same or similar consciousness as in humans[^14], and Anthropic itself tells us that such systems could threaten humanity. We should take them at their word. They are, after all, one of only a handful of transformative companies. And if we do, 
> Of what use is a new treatment for cancer if the remaining scraps of humanity are living in an A.I.-generated wasteland?
>
>-- @2026french

[^14]: The prospectus does not use the word consciousness, but it does acknowledge 'emerging debates regarding whether AI systems possess or could develop forms of sentience or moral status', and refers to 'the way we approach model sentience and moral status'.


I started the discussion with the existential angst experienced by Gilgamesh facing his own mortality. From those ancient times to today, a central question, perhaps *the* central question, is: how do we cheat death? It remains unanswered.
