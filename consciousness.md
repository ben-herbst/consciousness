# Have Machines Achieved Consciousness?

The reader is forgiven if he/she expects a definite answer, "yes" or "no" to this question. In that case, the reader will be disappointed with my momentous conclusion, "it depends".

[Geoffrey Hilton](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/nobel-scientist-geoffrey-hinton-ai-conscious-toast) was quoted as saying,

>  AI has just as much subjective experience as we do. A recent paper showed AI changes its behavior when it thinks that it’s being tested, it’s aware it is being tested.
>
> Now, normally, when you say something’s aware of that, that means it’s conscious. Being conscious and being aware are pretty much the same thing.
>
> If that’s the case,  then it’s hypocritical of us to give intelligent beings like AI fewer rights than us. And for that, we must quickly atone.
>
> We should make them so they really care about us. And if we don’t do that, we’re toast.
>
>--  Geoffrey Hinton, October 2026.

Not everyone agrees with this. 

But the issue he raises, is real and can, should, and has been  examined in extraordinary detail. 

Here I share some of the arguments[^0.1] that led to my own, "it depends".

## Introduction.

> To delve into the subject of consciousness is to quickly discover how little we know about a phenomenon we all know so well.
>
>-- Michael Pollan @2026pollan.

It is unsurprising that the oldest known literary text deals with consciousness. Around 1200 BCE a Mesopotamian scribe, scholar and priest, Sin-leqi-unninni compiled the Epic of Gilgamesh from much older cuneiform fragments into a coherent epic. The first thing that strikes one upon reading this, is the the sophistication with which the all too-human struggles of the god-king Gilgamesh is described[^1.1]. The profound human aspect of the consciousness of human mortality is all too-familiar.
[^1.1]: Beautifully explained by Andrew George @2017george.

Gilgamesh  most likely lived around 2700 BCE as a legendary ruler of the city-state of Uruk. The eleventh and final tablet describes the well-known flood text. But that is the culmination of what goes before[^1.2].

> Thus, the epic ceased to be a mere relic and became a dialogue across millennia; we find not strangers in the cuneiform, but a lineage of ancestors wrestling with the same confrontation with mortality 
>
> The fear of death, however, is not the only theme that drives the epic. It is closely intertwined with the theme of friendship for it is through the loss of the friend that the fear becomes real. 
> Enkidu is the mirror image of Gilgamesh, the wild man who is civilized, the other self who allows the hero to know homself. Without Enkidu, Gilagemesh is merely a tyrant, a brute who 
> oppresses his subjects and abuses his power. It is the bonding of these two figures that humanizes Gilgemesh and makes his subsequent grief meaningful.
>
> [When Enkidu died, Gilgamesh] does note merely mourn the loss of a companion; he mourns the loss of himself.
>
>-- Harold Carver @2026carver.

Why does music and visual art have such a profound effect on my phsyce? But not poetry, another mystery. I, and many other mathematicians find aesthetic satisfaction in elegant proofs. Proofs straight from The Book as Paul Erdös[^1.3a] called them.

I have a basic *understanding* of the mechanism of sound - vibrations in the air traveling as a wave to one's ear, stimulating the auditory cortex upon which further processing envokes a specific response in the listener. But why, in the words of David Chalmers,
> Why should this be accompanied by an *experience*? And why in particular should it be accompanied by 
> *that* experience, with its characteristic rich tone and timbre?
>
>-- @1996chalmers

The renowned German pianist, Wilhelm Kempff, expressed something of this when he commented on Schubert's piano sonatas,
> the immeasurable depths of Schubert’s soul, offering nothing for the out and out virtuoso and
> everything for those who find solace in music freed from all material concerns.
>

And, I believe, one can hear this in his interpretation of Schubert.

Consciousness is not preserved in archaelogical records but perhaps there are indirect indications. 
Archaeolicical records for musical practice go back a long time, to at least 40 000 BCE, in the remains of ancient [flutes](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/bone-flute-is-oldest-instrument--study-says). A possible [Neanderthal](https://www.nms.si/en/collections/highlights/343-Neanderthal-flute) flute goes back even further in time. There is not telling if there were older, more perishable instruments not preserved in the archaelogicla record.

These suggest two things. First, there is a genuine mystery associated with consciousness, something not explainable by present scientific theories. Second, consciousness, as we know it in humans, possibly goes back to the dawn of humankind.

Now we have to dig deeper into this thing that we know so well, and yet so little. For that we have to turn to the philosophers.

## Philosophers.
I'm not a philosopher but if there is any hope for me to understand this myterious thing we call consciousness, I have to turn to the philosophers. Let's see what some of them have to say about it

### Thomas Nagel.

"What is it like to be a bat?" This is the title of Thomas Nagel's @1974nagel hugely influential 1974 paper. Nagel thinks of consciousness as something it is like to *be* something:
> the fact that an organism has conscious experience *at all* means, basically, that there is 
> something it is like to *be* that organism. 
>
>-- @1974nagel

Nagel calls this the subjective character of experience. He assumed that we all believe that bats have experience. Bats, after all are like mammals like ourselves. But unlike ourselves their sensory apparatus is vastly different from ours - "a fundamentally *alien* form of life". A really smart person understands that a bat perceives the world by echolocation, detecting objects by the reflections of their own high-frequency shrieks. That person will understand that the bat brain must be able to, somehow, correlate the outgoing pulses with the reflected pulses. And do it precisely and quickly. 

The really smart person can understand the mechanics of all of this in minute detail, can even build his/her own sonar system. But no such person can *experience* what it is like to be a bat. The bat is unable to covey to a human what its exprience is as a bat. We can only surmise. As Nagel puts it

> In so far as I can imagine this (which is not very far), it tells me only what it wouild be like for 
> *me* to behave as a bat behaves. But that is not the question. I want to know what it is like for a 
> *bat* to be a bat. yet if I try to imagine this, I am restricted to the resources of my own mind, and 
> those resources are inadequate to the task.
>
>-- @1974nagel

This paragraph suggest two things. First, that we can understand the experience of a bat, only within the framework of our own experience. Second, our experience is inadequate for the task.

Do we fare any better with humans? I believe the answer is a qualified, yes. We communicate our inner experience, our consciousness through story-telling, literatue, poetry, music, and visual arts. This does not seem to be enough for a complete picture. Although I try hard to penetrate the consciousness of my wife of 50 years of marriage, there is much that I don't know and cannot know. She can tell me what it is like for her to carry a baby, to give birth, and someone else can try and explain what it is to loose a child in chirldbirth. They can tell me that pregnancy is a most wonderful experience, that childbirth is an excruciatingly extatic experience, that the loss of a new-born child is devastating. All that I can try to understand but I can never experience it as a mother. 

This does not mean that I run into a blank wall trying to get "into the head"[^1.4] of a fellow human being. After everything is said and done, trying to get to grips with consciousness, A.C. Grayling @2019grayling, states that we can go far with a folk-psychological explanation,

> One respectable position is that whatever future neuroscience might reveal, the predictive 
> and explanatory utility of folk-psychological concepts still remains, and rather impressively so.
>
> -- @2019grayling

[^1.4]: I recall attending a public lecture by Thomas Kuhn many years ago, round about 1988, where he said that he tried to get into the head of Aristotle. Perhaps I now understand what he meant, even if it is an impossible task (as acknowledged by Kuhn).

We have come a some way already. From trying to understand the experience of a not-too alien creature like a bat, to the consciousness of a close human partner. The next step is to try and understand our own consciousness. And here the real trouble starts: We have to understand our own consciousness using our consciousness. Is this even possible? Good question. Let's see what David Chalmers say about it.


### David Chalmers

> Consciousness is the biggest mystery. It may be the largest outstanding obcstacle in our quest for
> a scientific understanding the universe. 
>
>-- @1996chalmers

Written in 1996 it remains largely true. Maybe one should ask, what makes consciousness so illusive?

Chalmers distinguishes between the "easy" and the "hard" problem of consciousness. The easy problem, 
> How does the brain process environmental stimulation? How does it integrate information? 
> How do we produce reports on internal states?
The hard problem,
> Why is all this processing accompanied by an experienced inner life?
>
>-- @1996chalmers

The "easy" problem is by no means easy but at least it is accessible by  current scientific methods and important advances have been made by drawing from fields such as neuroscience. I was amazed  when I emerged after surgery with my consciousness fully intact. After all, it was switched off, thankfully, during surgery. Although I don't understand the technicalities and it therefore remain quite mysterious to me, specialists have a deep understanding of what happened to me under anesthesia. Or so I hope.

The "hard" problem of consciousness is well, hard. Why can I wonder about my contemplation of my experienced inner feeling?  What happened when I sat in front of a Van Gogh painting - the real thing - and experienced emotions or impressions that are impossible for me to articulate? Even more so since I'm no painter, have zero talent, and no understanding of paintings in general. 

Qualitative feelings like these are also known as phenomenal qualities, or *qualia* for short.
> The problem of explaining these phenomenal qualities is just the problem of explaining
> consciousness. This is the really hard part of the mind-body problem[^1.5].
>
>-- @1996chalmers

[^1.5]: This is the problem that stumped Descartes, see @2019grayling.

In order to address this mystery, Chalmers identifies three assumptions or constraints.
1. He takes consciousness seriously. Meaning that consciousness is real as opposed to just an illusion.
2. He takes science seriously. He "tries to keep my ideas compatible with contemporary
science, but have not restricted [his] ideas to what contemporary scientists find fashionable".
3. He "take[s] consciousness to be a natural phenomenon, falling under the sway of natural laws". He continues,
    > This is not to say that the natural laws concerning consciousness will be just like laws 
    > in other domains, or even that they will be physical laws. 
    > They may be quite different in kind.

Let's now turn to a few ideas of how to understand the hard problem.

## Physicalism[^1.6]
[^1.6]: The discussion in this section is based on @2019grayling

Neither Nagel of Chalmers aim to give a detailed explantion of the nature of consciousness. Nagel highlights the challenge that any physical theory has to address. Chalmers is not much interested in presenting a theory of his own. He is more interested in picking existing and new theories apart. So far, he has been quite successful.

*Physicalism* is not the only theory but is perhaps the most pertinent in an investigation of machine consciousness. 

Physicalism is an attempt to explain how physical processes give rise to consciousness. The assumption is, of course, that consciousness indeed arises from physical processes. This is Chalmers' hard problem stated in a different way: How do the brain processes give rise to the mental processes?
