# Consciousness

[Geoffrey Hilton](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/nobel-scientist-geoffrey-hinton-ai-conscious-toast) was quoted as saying,

>  AI has just as much subjective experience as we do. A recent paper showed AI changes its behavior when it thinks that it’s being tested, it’s aware it is being tested.
>
> Now, normally, when you say something’s aware of that, that means it’s conscious. Being conscious and being aware are pretty much the same thing.
>
> If that’s the case,  then it’s hypocritical of us to give intelligent beings like AI fewer rights than us. And for that, we must quickly atone.
>
> We should make them so they really care about us. And if we don’t do that, we’re toast.
>
>--  Geoffrey Hinton, October 2026.

Not everyone agrees. But the issue he raises, whether  AI has or can develop human-like consciousness is real and can, should, and has been  examined in extraordinary detail. Here I share some of the arguments[^0.1], for and against.

## Preamble.

> To delve into the subject of consciousness is to quickly discover how little we know about a phenomenon we all know so well.
>
>-- Michael Pollan @2026pollan.

It is unsurprising that the oldest known literary text deals with consciousness. It was around 1200 BCE that a Mesopotamian scribe, scholar and priest, Sin-leqi-unninni compiled the Epic of Gilgamesh for much older cuneiform fragments into a coherent tale. Gilgamesh most likely lived around 2700 BCE as a legendary ruler of the city-state of Uruk. The eleventh and final tablet describes the well-known flood text. But that is only the culmination of a long struggle by Gilgamesh with his own mortality. As two-thirds god and one-third human, it was the human part caused all the trouble. What resonates with us through the millennia,  is the profound human aspect of the consciousness of mortality. Harold Carver @2026carver, notes, 

> Thus, the epic ceased to be a mere relic and became a dialogue across millennia; we find not strangers in the cuneiform, but a lineage of ancestors wrestling with the same confrontation with mortality 
>
> The fear of death, however, is not the only theme that drives the epic. It is closely intertwined with the theme of friendship for it is through the loss of the friend that the fear becomes real. 
> Enkidu is the mirror image of Gilgamesh, the wild man who is civilized, the other self who allows the hero to know homself. Without Enkidu, Gilagemesh is merely a tyrant, a brute who 
> oppresses his subjects and abuses his power. It is the bonding of these two figures that humanizes Gilgemesh and makes his subsequent grief meaningful.
>
> [When Enkidu died, Gilgamesh] does note merely mourn the loss of a companion; he mourns the loss of himself.
>
>-- Harold Carver @2026carver.

In the final tablet, Gilgamesh sets off to find Utnapishtim, the only survivor of the great flood and the only human ever granted immortality by the gods, to find the Plant of Rejuvenation. When this is ultimately unsuccessful, Gilgamesh is forced to accept that mortality is a condition to be accepted with dignity[^1.1] 
[^1.1]: It is interesting to note that the Sumerians settle for a pessimistic view of the human condition: Immortality has to be accepted and the only means to achieve some measure of it, is though deeds - conquest and buildings. Perhaps ironically that Gilgamesh attained immortality through the text on clay tablets. The Egyptians and many other cultures later on, settled on mortality through various forms of an after-life. Different solutions to the same mortal anxiety. A reality imprinted in different ways on the collective consciousness, if there is such thing.
